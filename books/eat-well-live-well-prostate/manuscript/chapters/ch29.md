# CHAPTER 29
## Sexual Health and Infection Prevention: STIs, Screening, and Safe Practices

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to: describe the sexually transmitted infection landscape relevant to men across the lifespan — bacterial, viral, and parasitic agents with their symptom profiles and treatment principles; apply the screening guidelines for HIV, hepatitis B and C, chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and HPV with risk-stratified frequency recommendations; explain the HPV-prostate connection's evidence status with calibrated honesty; evaluate barrier-method and prevention strategies including condom use, vaccination (HPV and hepatitis B), and PrEP for HIV prevention; navigate the disclosure, partner-notification, and treatment-adherence structures that infection management requires; and integrate infection prevention into this book's broader sexual-health architecture without stigma or complacency.

### Opening Perspective

Sexual health's infection dimension occupies an awkward position in men's health literature: universally relevant (every sexually active man's risk structure exists regardless of age, relationship-status, or self-image), systematically undertalked (the stigma's silence persisting across demographics, the midlife-and-older-man's-particular-blindspot where "safe sex" messaging was often youth-targeted and where new-partnership-epidemiology continues through divorce, bereavement, and relationship-transition), and medically significant for this book's audience specifically (the infections's-prostate-interfaces — prostatitis's infectious-causes from Chapter 32, the HPV's-prostate-cancer-association-question, the HIV's-immune-and-systemic-relationships — making infection-prevention-prostate-relevant beyond-the-STI's-direct-morbidity). This chapter treats the subject with the clinical precision and shame-free directness that the material requires: the pathogen-by-pathogen file, the screening-protocols, the prevention-strategies (barriers, vaccination, PrEP), the management-structures (testing, treatment, disclosure, partner-notification), and the integration-with-this-book's-sexual-health-architecture.

The demographic-specific-epidemiology deserves early statement: STI-incidence-rates in adults over 50 have risen across multiple-infection-categories in many countries (driven by relationship-transition-patterns, reduced-precaution-use ("I'm-too-old-for-condoms"-assumption), reduced-testing-optimism ("it's-not-a-young-person's-problem"), and the physiological-factors (age-related-mucosal-changes, comorbidity-burden, and-treatment-resistance-concerns in-some-cases), with the-midlife-man's-risk-structure-being-real-and-underrecognized — which-is-why-this-chapter's-messaging-standards-include-the-age-inclusivity from-the-start: prevention-and-screening-appropriate-throughout-sexual-life, testing-based-on-risk-rather-than-on-age-stereotypes, and-the-stigma's-defiance (the-man-who-gets-tested-is-practicing-responsible-sexual-health-management-at-any-age — which-is-this-chapter's-identity-reclamation: the-infection-prevention's-rebranding-from-youth-panic-to-lifelong-health-practice).

### The Immune System and Infection Resistance: The Host-Side Defense

Every infection story has two sides: the pathogen's properties and the host's defenses. This book's chapters have devoted substantial attention to the pathogens — their biology, their transmission, their treatment — but the host side deserves equal treatment, because the man's immune system is the first, the constant, and often the decisive participant in every infection encounter, and the lifestyle factors that this book's program modifies (the sleep, the stress, the nutrition, the alcohol, the smoking, the weight) each have documented effects on immune function that convert the daily-health program into an infection-prevention program of its own.

The innate immune system is the first responder, and its components are the barriers and patrols that this chapter's prevention section has already described at the behavioral level: the skin and mucous membranes (the physical barriers whose integrity depends on nutrition, hydration, and the avoidance of the irritants and injuries that breach them), the antimicrobial secretions (the saliva, the gastric acid, the urinary-flow's mechanical wash that Chapter 33's hydration-and-voiding habits maintain, the seminal fluid's own antimicrobial peptides that the sexual-health file describes), and the cellular patrols (the neutrophils, the macrophages, the natural killer cells that circulate continuously and respond to the invasion signals that infected tissue emits). The innate system's response speed — minutes to hours — makes it the decisive factor in whether an exposure becomes an infection: the pathogen that arrives at a well-maintained barrier-and-patrol system is typically eliminated before it establishes the foothold that the adaptive system would then need to address.
