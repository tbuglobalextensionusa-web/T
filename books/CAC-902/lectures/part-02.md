# Part II: Humanistic, Existential, and Behavioral Traditions

## Chapter 6: Horney and Fromm: Anxiety, Culture, and Character

Karen Horney and Erich Fromm extended psychodynamic thinking by insisting that culture and social conditions shape personality. They did not treat the individual as a sealed container of drives. They asked how family, gender, economic pressure, and social values create anxiety and then shape the ways people protect themselves. For counselors working with children and adolescents, this is a useful correction. A young person's distress may be a response to an environment that demands too much, offers too little safety, or confuses love with performance.

### Horney: basic anxiety and protective moves

Horney described basic anxiety as the feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world. A child who cannot rely on warmth may develop protective strategies. Horney grouped these into moving toward people, moving against people, and moving away from people. Moving toward seeks safety through compliance and affection. Moving against seeks safety through power and control. Moving away seeks safety through distance and self-sufficiency. These are not fixed personality types. They are strategies that can become rigid when anxiety is high.

A child who moves toward may be called sweet but may be terrified of disapproval. A child who moves against may be called defiant but may expect attack. A child who moves away may be called independent but may have given up on help. The counselor asks what the strategy protects and what it costs. The goal is not to remove the strategy by force. The goal is to make the child safer so that more flexible choices become possible.

### The tyranny of the should

Horney also described the tyranny of the should: internal demands that a person must be perfect, always loving, never needy, or always strong. These demands create a gap between the real self and an idealized image. The person may then hate the real self for failing to match the ideal. Adolescents are especially vulnerable because identity is forming and social comparison is intense. A teen may believe, "I should never feel jealous," and then feel shame when jealousy appears. The counselor can help the client separate a value from a tyrannical demand.

### Fromm: freedom, relatedness, and character

Erich Fromm argued that human beings need relatedness, transcendence, rootedness, identity, and a frame of orientation. Freedom can be experienced as both opportunity and burden. When people cannot bear aloneness, they may escape freedom through authoritarianism, destructiveness, or automatic conformity. Fromm's social character concept describes patterns shared by members of a society because they adapt to common conditions. A marketing culture may reward having over being. A school culture may reward ranking over learning. The counselor should not reduce social analysis to individual pathology.

Fromm distinguished productive and nonproductive orientations. A productive orientation involves love, reason, and creative participation. Nonproductive orientations include receptive, exploitative, hoarding, and marketing styles. These are not diagnoses. They are lenses for asking how a person relates to the world. A teenager who treats the self as a product to be optimized may be expressing a marketing orientation. The counselor can ask, "If you were not being evaluated, what would you want?"

### Clinical example

Sixteen-year-old Tolu is exhausted by extracurricular achievements. Her parents say they only want her to be happy, but every conversation returns to university admission. Tolu has headaches and says she feels empty. A Horneyan formulation might note moving toward: Tolu complies to keep love. A Frommian formulation might note a marketing self: Tolu experiences herself as a résumé. The counselor does not blame the parents. The counselor helps the family notice the pressure cycle and helps Tolu identify values that are not performance-based.

Intervention may include family sessions, values clarification, and small acts of self-definition. Tolu might drop one activity and observe the family response. The experiment tests whether love survives imperfection. The counselor also checks for depression and safety. Cultural critique does not replace clinical assessment.

### Gender and culture

Horney challenged Freud's views of women, arguing that cultural restrictions, not biology alone, shape feminine psychology. She criticized penis envy as a male-centered construct and emphasized the impact of gender inequality. This critique remains relevant when counselors work with girls who are shamed for anger, boys who are shamed for vulnerability, or gender-diverse youth who are forced into narrow roles. The counselor should ask whose standards define health. A quiet girl may be praised while her needs are ignored. An aggressive boy may be punished while his fear is ignored.

### Limits and integration

Horney and Fromm offer rich clinical language but less standardized treatment protocols than CBT. Their ideas can become vague if every problem is called cultural. The counselor should connect social analysis to specific mechanisms: family rules, peer norms, economic stress, discrimination, or school pressure. Integration is natural. A counselor may use Horney's protective moves in formulation, CBT for panic symptoms, and family therapy to change the pressure system.

### Reflection

Write three shoulds you carry. For each, ask who taught it, what it protects, and what it costs. Then ask what value might remain if the tyranny were removed. This exercise is not about rejecting all standards. It is about distinguishing chosen values from fear-based demands.

## Chapter 7: Jung and Analytical Psychology

Carl Jung's analytical psychology introduces a different emphasis from Freud's. Jung focused on the collective unconscious, archetypes, individuation, and the symbolic life. His ideas have influenced art, religion, literature, and psychotherapy. They are also controversial. A doctoral counselor should approach Jung with both openness and critical discipline. Symbolic work can be meaningful for some clients, but archetypal claims should not be presented as settled empirical science.

### Persona, shadow, and self

Jung described the persona as the social mask a person presents to the world. The persona is necessary; every society requires roles. Problems arise when a person identifies completely with the mask and loses contact with other parts of the self. The shadow contains qualities the ego rejects or does not recognize. The shadow is not only evil. It may contain creativity, anger, tenderness, or ambition that the person was taught to hide. The Self, in Jung's theory, is an organizing center of the whole psyche, often represented in symbols of wholeness.

A teenager who is always the helper may have a shadow full of unmet need. A teenager who is always tough may have a shadow full of fear. The counselor does not announce, "This is your shadow." The counselor invites the client to notice what is excluded. "What part of you is not allowed in this story?" The question can open space without imposing a symbol.

### Archetypes and the collective unconscious

Archetypes are proposed universal patterns of image and behavior, such as the hero, the mother, the trickster, or the wise old person. Jung argued that they arise from the collective unconscious, a layer of psyche shared by humanity. This claim is difficult to test scientifically. A counselor can use archetypal images as metaphors without asserting that they are biologically inherited structures. A client may find the hero story useful for thinking about courage. Another client may find it oppressive because it demands constant bravery. The meaning belongs to the client.

### Individuation

Individuation is the process of becoming a whole person, integrating conscious and unconscious aspects rather than remaining split. It is not the same as individualism. Jung did not mean selfish isolation. He meant a more complete relationship with oneself and the world. In adolescence, identity formation can be seen as an individuation process, but counselors should not force Jung's stages onto every teen. Cultural traditions differ in how they understand selfhood. Some emphasize relational identity more than separate identity.

### Dreams and active imagination

Jung placed great weight on dreams. He saw them as compensatory, offering what consciousness lacks. A client who feels powerful may dream of being small. A client who feels hopeless may dream of a hidden door. The counselor explores the dream's images, emotions, and associations rather than applying a fixed dictionary. Active imagination is a waking practice of engaging inner images through writing, drawing, or dialogue. It can be useful for creative clients but may be destabilizing for clients with psychosis or severe dissociation unless carefully adapted and supervised.

### Clinical example

Seventeen-year-old Priya draws a recurring image of a walled garden. In therapy, she associates the wall with family protection and the garden with a part of herself no one sees. A Jungian lens might explore the symbol of enclosed potential. A person-centered counselor would follow Priya's meaning without naming archetypes. A CBT counselor might explore beliefs about exposure and judgment. The counselor can integrate: use the image as a shared metaphor, ask what the gate would need to open safely, and connect the metaphor to real-life steps.

The risk is overinterpretation. If the counselor says, "The garden is the Great Mother archetype," Priya may feel invaded. The counselor should ask, "What does the garden mean to you?" The client's association is primary.

### Evidence and caution

Jungian therapy has a smaller empirical base than CBT for specific disorders. Some clients report meaning and growth, but meaning is not the same as symptom reduction. Counselors should not use Jungian ideas to bypass diagnosis, safety planning, or evidence-based treatment when those are needed. Spiritual emergency language should not be used to dismiss psychosis. Cultural humility is essential because symbols do not mean the same thing across traditions.

### Reflection

Choose a recurring image from a dream, story, or drawing. Do not interpret it immediately. Write what it feels like, what it protects, and what it might invite. Then ask what evidence would support or challenge your interpretation. Symbolic work is strongest when it remains curious.

## Chapter 8: Person-Centered Therapy: Rogers and Maslow

Carl Rogers developed person-centered therapy around a radical proposition: the client has a formative tendency toward growth, and the therapist's task is to provide conditions in which that tendency can operate. The core conditions are congruence, unconditional positive regard, and empathic understanding. Rogers did not see these as polite extras. He argued that they are central to therapeutic change. Abraham Maslow, working in a related humanistic tradition, studied self-actualization, peak experiences, and human motivation.

### The core conditions in practice

Congruence means the therapist is real and transparent within professional limits. Unconditional positive regard means the therapist values the client without making worth conditional on behavior. Empathy means the therapist accurately senses the client's inner frame of reference and communicates that understanding. In practice, these conditions are not passive. The therapist must listen carefully, check understanding, and remain present when the client's experience is confusing or painful.

A child-centered application might look like play therapy in which the counselor follows the child's lead, reflects feeling, and sets limits only when needed for safety. The counselor does not direct the play toward a predetermined lesson. The child uses play to express what cannot yet be said. A counselor might say, "That tower keeps falling, and you look frustrated." The reflection is not a technique to produce insight. It is a way of being with the child's experience.

### Conditions of worth and the self-concept

Rogers argued that children need positive regard. When regard is conditional, children may develop conditions of worth: "I am lovable only if I am quiet," or "I am acceptable only if I achieve." The self-concept then becomes organized around approval rather than organismic experience. Incongruence arises between the self-concept and lived experience. A teen who believes "I never get angry" may experience rage as threatening and distort it. Therapy offers a relationship in which the experience can be admitted without losing love.

### Maslow and motivation

Maslow's hierarchy of needs is widely known but often oversimplified. The idea that lower needs must be fully satisfied before higher needs appear is not a rigid law. People seek meaning, love, and creativity even in hardship. Still, the hierarchy reminds counselors that safety, food, sleep, and belonging matter. A child cannot use a cognitive worksheet effectively when hungry or terrified. Doctoral assessment includes basic needs and social context.

Self-actualization refers to the realization of potential. Maslow studied people he considered psychologically healthy and described qualities such as acceptance, spontaneity, problem-centering, and peak experiences. Critics note that his sample was selective and culturally shaped. The counselor should use the idea as an invitation to growth, not as a standard to judge clients.

### Nondirectivity and its limits

Person-centered therapy is often called nondirective. This does not mean the therapist has no structure. The frame, confidentiality, and limits are structured. Nondirectivity means the therapist does not impose an agenda on the client's experience. With young children, some direction may be needed for safety. With adolescents, a purely nondirective stance may be experienced as indifference if the teen wants help with a concrete problem. The counselor can remain person-centered while offering a rationale and collaborating on tasks.

### Evidence and application

The therapeutic relationship is one of the most consistently supported factors in psychotherapy outcome. Person-centered therapy has evidence for various concerns, and its core conditions influence many other approaches. However, for some specific disorders, structured treatments such as exposure-based CBT have stronger symptom-specific evidence. A counselor can honor Rogers while using structured methods. The question is whether the method is offered within a relationship of respect and collaboration.

### Clinical example

Eleven-year-old Sam is referred for selective mutism. A directive plan that pressures speech may increase anxiety. A person-centered counselor builds safety through play, accepts nonverbal communication, and does not make speech a condition of regard. At the same time, the counselor may collaborate with a behavioral plan for gradual communication. The person-centered condition protects the child's dignity; the behavioral plan provides steps. The child is not a project to be fixed. The child is a person whose voice will emerge when safety and skill meet.

### Reflection

Think of a relationship in which you felt accepted even when you failed. What did that acceptance make possible? Then think of a relationship in which you felt valued only when you performed. How did your behavior change? Clients bring these histories into the room. The counselor's task is to offer a different condition.

## Chapter 9: Existential Therapy: Frankl, May, and Meaning

Existential therapy focuses on the conditions of human existence: freedom, responsibility, isolation, meaning, and death. It is less a set of techniques than a stance toward the client's life. Viktor Frankl developed logotherapy after surviving Nazi concentration camps. Rollo May brought existential ideas into American psychology. Irvin Yalom later organized existential concerns into four ultimate concerns: death, freedom, existential isolation, and meaninglessness. For adolescents, these concerns are not abstract. They appear as questions about identity, fairness, mortality, and whether effort matters.

### Frankl and meaning

Frankl argued that the primary motivational force is the will to meaning. Meaning can be found through creative work, experience, and attitude toward unavoidable suffering. This does not mean suffering is good or that clients should accept abuse. Frankl's point is that even when circumstances cannot be changed, a person may retain some freedom in how they respond. The counselor must be careful not to use meaning language to silence protest. A child in an unsafe home needs protection, not a lecture about attitude.

Logotherapy uses techniques such as dereflection and paradoxical intention in selected cases. Dereflection shifts attention from hyperreflection to meaningful action. Paradoxical intention invites the client to wish for the feared symptom in a way that disrupts anticipatory anxiety. These techniques require skill and are not appropriate for every client. With children, meaning work is usually concrete: caring for a pet, helping a sibling, creating art, or choosing a small act of courage.

### May and anxiety

Rollo May distinguished normal anxiety from neurotic anxiety. Normal anxiety is part of growth and freedom. Neurotic anxiety is disproportionate, repressed, and managed through rigid defenses. May argued that avoiding all anxiety can shrink the self. The counselor helps the client face the anxiety that belongs to living. For an adolescent leaving home, anxiety may be a sign of meaningful transition. For a child with panic attacks, anxiety may need symptom-focused treatment first. The existential lens does not replace assessment.

### Freedom and responsibility

Existential therapy emphasizes that people are always choosing, even when choices are constrained. Responsibility does not mean blaming the client for oppression. It means helping the client notice the responses that remain possible. A teenager in poverty did not choose poverty. The teenager can still choose how to seek support, what values to protect, and what future to work toward. The counselor must hold both realities: structural injustice and personal agency.

### Existential isolation

Existential isolation refers to the unbridgeable gap between persons. No one can fully live another's life. This can be frightening, but it also makes relationship meaningful. Adolescents often feel that no one understands them. The counselor does not claim total understanding. The counselor says, "I cannot know exactly what it is like, and I want to understand as much as you can show me." That honesty can be more connecting than false certainty.

### Death and finitude

Children encounter death through pets, relatives, news, and illness. Avoiding the topic can increase fear. Existential work helps the client face finitude in developmentally appropriate ways. A child may ask, "Will you die?" The counselor answers honestly and gently, then explores the fear. With adolescents, death concerns may appear in risk-taking, nihilism, or intense activism. The counselor listens for the meaning question beneath the behavior.

### Clinical example

Eighteen-year-old Aisha says, "Nothing matters. We're all going to die anyway." A purely cognitive response might challenge the thought as distorted. An existential response first validates the question: "You are looking at the big picture and wondering why any of it counts." The counselor then explores what Aisha values, what relationships matter, and what actions feel consistent with those values. If depression is present, the counselor also assesses safety and may use CBT or other evidence-based treatment. Existential dialogue and symptom treatment can coexist.

### Limits and reflection

Existential therapy can be vague if it stays at the level of philosophy. It can also be culturally narrow if it assumes that individual choice is the highest value. Some clients find meaning in community, tradition, or religious faith. The counselor should follow the client's frame. Reflection: Write about a moment when you faced a limit you could not change. What response remained possible? What did that moment teach you about freedom?

## Chapter 10: Behavior Therapy and Learning Principles

Behavior therapy begins with a simple but powerful idea: behavior is learned in context, and changing the context can change the behavior. This does not mean that thoughts, feelings, or culture are irrelevant. It means that the counselor starts with observable patterns and the conditions that maintain them. For children and adolescents, behavior therapy is often practical because it can involve caregivers, teachers, and daily routines. It also has a strong evidence base for anxiety, disruptive behavior, and habit problems.

### Classical conditioning

Classical conditioning occurs when a neutral stimulus becomes associated with a response. A child who is bitten by a dog may fear all dogs. A student who is humiliated in math class may feel panic when entering the room. The fear is not a choice. It is a learned association. Exposure-based treatment works by creating new learning: the feared stimulus is encountered without the expected catastrophe, and the body learns safety. Avoidance prevents this learning.

### Operant conditioning

Operant conditioning focuses on consequences. Behavior that is reinforced tends to increase. Behavior that is not reinforced or is punished tends to decrease. Positive reinforcement adds something valued. Negative reinforcement removes something aversive. Punishment adds something aversive or removes something valued. These terms are often confused. A child who tantrums and then receives a tablet has been positively reinforced. A child who tantrums and escapes a demand has been negatively reinforced. Both can increase tantrums, but the mechanism differs.

### Functional assessment

A functional assessment asks what happens before and after a behavior. Antecedents include setting events, triggers, and demands. Consequences include attention, escape, tangibles, and sensory effects. The counselor forms a hypothesis about function. A behavior that looks the same may serve different functions. One child hits to escape work; another hits to gain peer attention. The intervention should match the function. Teaching a replacement behavior is usually more effective than punishment alone.

### Caregiver and school application

Behavioral parent training teaches caregivers to give clear instructions, reinforce desired behavior, use consistent consequences, and reduce coercive cycles. A coercive cycle occurs when a parent makes a demand, a child escalates, the parent withdraws the demand, and the child's escalation is reinforced. The parent also learns that giving in stops the noise, so both parties are reinforced. Breaking the cycle requires calm consistency and reinforcement of cooperation.

In school, a behavior plan may include a token system, a break card, or a check-in/check-out routine. The plan should be simple, measurable, and reviewed. If the plan fails, the counselor asks whether the function was wrong, the reinforcer was weak, the demand was too hard, or the context was unsafe. Failure is data, not proof that the child is bad.

### Exposure and anxiety

Exposure is a central behavioral treatment for anxiety. The client faces feared stimuli in a planned, gradual way while reducing safety behaviors. A hierarchy orders fears from easier to harder. The goal is not to eliminate anxiety instantly. The goal is to learn that anxiety can be tolerated and that the feared outcome does not occur or is manageable. With children, exposure is often playful. A child afraid of dogs may start with pictures, then videos, then a calm dog at a distance. The child's consent and pacing matter.

### Clinical example

Eight-year-old Noah refuses to sleep alone. Each night he cries, and a parent lies with him until he sleeps. The parent is exhausted. A behavioral formulation notes that crying produces parental presence, which reduces Noah's fear and reinforces crying. The parent's presence also reinforces the parent's escape from distress. The plan includes a bedtime routine, gradual fading of parental presence, and reinforcement for staying in bed. The counselor also assesses for trauma and anxiety. If Noah has nightmares from abuse, exposure alone is not enough. Safety and trauma treatment come first.

### Ethics and limits

Behavior therapy has been criticized for controlling clients. The ethical question is who chooses the goal and whether the goal serves the client's welfare. A plan to make a child silent for adult convenience is not ethical. A plan to help a child communicate needs is ethical. Reinforcement should not be humiliating. Punishment should be avoided when possible. The counselor should consider neurodiversity. Some behaviors, such as stimming, may be self-regulating and should not be extinguished simply because they look unusual.

### Reflection

Choose a small behavior you want to change. Identify antecedents, consequences, and function. Design a replacement behavior and a reinforcement plan. Then ask whether the goal is truly yours or imposed by someone else. Behavior therapy works best when the goal is meaningful to the person whose behavior is changing.
