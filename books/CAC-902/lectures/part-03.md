# Part III: Cognitive, Contextual, and Systemic Traditions

## Chapter 11: Cognitive Therapy and CBT

Cognitive therapy, associated with Aaron Beck, proposes that emotional distress is shaped not only by events but by the meanings people give to events. Cognitive-behavioral therapy, or CBT, combines this cognitive focus with behavioral methods. It is one of the most researched forms of psychotherapy. For child and adolescent counseling, CBT is often adapted to be concrete, active, and developmentally appropriate. A worksheet is not CBT. The core is collaborative empiricism: therapist and client examine thoughts, feelings, behaviors, and evidence together.

### The cognitive model

A situation triggers an automatic thought, which influences emotion and behavior. A student receives a low grade and thinks, "I'm stupid." The thought produces shame and avoidance. Another student thinks, "I need a different study plan," and feels disappointment but seeks help. The event is similar; the meaning differs. Cognitive therapy does not say that thoughts cause everything. It says that interpretations are one leverage point. In children, automatic thoughts may be images rather than words. A child may see a picture of being laughed at rather than hear a sentence.

### Cognitive distortions and core beliefs

Common distortions include all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophizing, mind reading, overgeneralization, emotional reasoning, and personalization. These are not lies. They are habitual shortcuts that become problematic when they are rigid. Core beliefs are deeper assumptions about self, others, and the world. A child with a core belief "I am unlovable" may interpret a parent's lateness as rejection. The counselor does not argue the child out of the belief. The counselor helps the child notice the belief, test it, and build alternative experiences.

### Collaborative empiricism and Socratic questions

The counselor asks questions that help the client evaluate thoughts. "What evidence supports that idea? What evidence does not fit? What would you tell a friend? If the worst happened, how would you cope?" The tone is curious, not cross-examining. With young children, the counselor may use drawings, puppets, or a detective game. The child becomes a scientist of their own mind. The counselor should avoid turning Socratic questioning into a debate the counselor must win. If the client says, "I know it's true," the counselor can explore the feeling and the history rather than push harder.

### Behavioral experiments

A behavioral experiment tests a prediction in real life. A socially anxious teen predicts that if they ask a question in class, everyone will laugh. The counselor and teen design a small test: ask one question in a supportive class and record what happens. The experiment is not a trick to prove the counselor right. It is a way to gather data. If peers do laugh, the counselor helps the teen cope and reevaluate the meaning. The goal is flexible learning, not forced positivity.

### CBT with children and adolescents

Developmental adaptation is essential. Younger children may need parent involvement, play, and visual aids. Adolescents may need autonomy, privacy, and relevance to their goals. CBT for anxiety often includes exposure. CBT for depression often includes behavioral activation, cognitive work, and problem-solving. CBT for trauma may include trauma narrative and processing. The counselor should match the protocol to the problem and monitor outcome. A generic CBT package is not enough.

### Clinical example

Thirteen-year-old Lina has panic attacks at school. She believes a racing heart means she will faint. The counselor teaches the panic cycle, then designs interoceptive exposure: safely running in place to produce a racing heart and learn that it is uncomfortable but not dangerous. Lina also tests the prediction that leaving class prevents fainting. The counselor involves the school so that escape is reduced gradually and safely. Cognitive work and behavioral exposure reinforce each other. Lina learns, "My body can be loud and I can still be safe."

### Evidence and limits

CBT has strong evidence for many anxiety and depressive disorders in youth, but effects are not universal. Some clients do not respond, and some protocols are less effective for complex trauma or comorbid conditions. CBT can be misused as a way to tell clients to think positively while ignoring poverty, racism, or abuse. A good CBT counselor validates real threats and targets distorted appraisals only where they exist. The counselor also considers whether the environment must change. Cognitive restructuring cannot make an unsafe home safe.

### Reflection

Recall a recent strong emotion. Write the situation, the automatic thought, the emotion, and the behavior. Then ask what evidence supports and challenges the thought. Finally, design a small experiment. Notice whether the exercise feels curious or judgmental. If it feels judgmental, slow down and add compassion. CBT is not about winning an argument with yourself.

## Chapter 12: Social Cognitive Theory and Self-Efficacy

Albert Bandura's social cognitive theory emphasizes learning through observation, the role of cognition, and the capacity for self-regulation. A central concept is self-efficacy: the belief that one can organize and execute the actions needed to manage a situation. Self-efficacy is not self-esteem. A child may like themselves but doubt their ability to solve math problems. Another child may have low self-worth but high efficacy in sports. Counseling often targets efficacy because it predicts effort, persistence, and recovery from setbacks.

### Observational learning

Children learn by watching models. A child who sees a parent handle frustration calmly may learn a regulation strategy. A child who sees aggression rewarded may learn aggression. Modeling is not imitation alone. The observer attends, retains, reproduces, and is motivated. Counselors can use coping models, not just mastery models. A coping model shows struggle and gradual success. A mastery model shows flawless performance. For anxious children, a coping model may be more believable.

### Sources of self-efficacy

Bandura described four main sources: mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states. Mastery experiences are the most powerful. A child who succeeds at a small step gains evidence of capability. Vicarious experience comes from seeing similar others succeed. Verbal persuasion can help if it is credible. Physiological states matter because a racing heart may be interpreted as danger or excitement. The counselor can help the client reinterpret arousal and build mastery steps.

### Self-regulation and agency

Social cognitive theory views people as agents who set goals, monitor progress, and adjust behavior. Self-regulation includes self-observation, judgment, and self-response. A teen who tracks study time, compares it with a goal, and rewards effort is using self-regulation. The counselor should avoid turning self-regulation into self-blame. If a plan fails, the counselor asks about barriers, skills, and environment. Agency is embedded in social conditions. A child cannot regulate their way out of food insecurity.

### Reciprocal determinism

Bandura proposed reciprocal determinism: behavior, personal factors, and environment influence one another. A child's anxiety leads to avoidance; avoidance reduces peer contact; reduced contact increases anxiety. Changing any part can shift the system. The counselor may target behavior through exposure, cognition through reappraisal, or environment through school accommodation. The theory supports multi-level intervention.

### Clinical example

Sixteen-year-old Marcus wants to join a robotics club but believes he is not smart enough. The counselor uses social cognitive principles. They identify a coping model: an older student who struggled at first. They break participation into mastery steps: attend one meeting, ask one question, build one small part. Marcus tracks his predictions and outcomes. The counselor also addresses the school environment by asking the club sponsor to welcome newcomers. Marcus's efficacy grows because he accumulates evidence, not because the counselor says, "Believe in yourself."

### Media and digital models

Adolescents learn from online models. Social media can provide supportive communities or harmful comparison. A teen who sees curated images may develop low efficacy about their own body or life. Counselors can teach critical media literacy: Who is the model? What is edited? What is the incentive? The goal is not to ban media but to increase agency in interpreting it.

### Evidence and limits

Self-efficacy is a robust predictor in many domains, but it is not a cure-all. High efficacy without skill can lead to failure. Low efficacy may be realistic in an unsafe environment. The counselor should build actual competence and safety, not just confidence. Social cognitive theory integrates well with CBT, behavioral parent training, and school consultation.

### Reflection

Choose a goal you have avoided. Rate your efficacy from zero to ten. Identify one mastery step, one model, one source of encouragement, and one way to reinterpret arousal. Then take the step and record what you learn. Efficacy changes through experience.

## Chapter 13: Contextual and Mindfulness-Based Approaches

A family of approaches sometimes called the third wave of behavior therapy emphasizes context, acceptance, mindfulness, values, and the relationship to thoughts rather than only the content of thoughts. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, or ACT, Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, and mindfulness-based interventions are prominent examples. They do not reject behavior change. They ask a different question: instead of trying to eliminate every difficult thought or feeling, can the client make room for experience while moving toward what matters?

### ACT and psychological flexibility

ACT targets psychological flexibility: the ability to contact the present moment and choose behavior aligned with values. Six processes are often described: acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values, and committed action. Cognitive defusion means noticing thoughts as mental events rather than literal truths. A teen may say, "I'm a failure." Defusion invites, "I am noticing the thought that I'm a failure." The thought may remain, but its grip can loosen.

Acceptance is not resignation. It is willingness to experience what is present when struggle against it causes harm. A child with chronic pain may learn to carry pain while still attending school and friends. Values are chosen directions, not goals to check off. A value of kindness can guide many actions. Committed action translates values into steps.

### DBT and dialectics

DBT was developed by Marsha Linehan for borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidality. It combines acceptance and change. Dialectics means holding opposites: I accept myself as I am, and I need to change. DBT includes individual therapy, skills training, phone coaching, and therapist consultation in its standard form. Skills include mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. Adaptations exist for adolescents, including family skills. DBT should not be reduced to a few worksheets. Safety planning and fidelity matter.

### Mindfulness-based approaches

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy use formal and informal mindfulness practices. The client learns to observe experience with curiosity and reduced reactivity. Mindfulness is not emptying the mind. It is noticing when attention wanders and returning. For trauma survivors, mindfulness can be destabilizing if it forces attention inward without safety. Trauma-sensitive modifications include open eyes, external anchors, choice, and short practices.

### Contextual behavioral science

These approaches often draw on functional contextualism: behavior is understood in context, and the truth of an analysis is judged by whether it helps achieve a purpose. The counselor asks what function a behavior serves. Worry may function as avoidance of deeper emotion. Self-criticism may function as an attempt to prevent rejection. The intervention targets the function, not just the form.

### Clinical example

Seventeen-year-old Rina has recurrent self-critical thoughts and avoids applying to college. ACT work helps her notice the thought "I don't belong" as a mental event. She clarifies a value of learning. She commits to one application step while allowing anxiety to be present. The counselor does not promise the thought will disappear. The goal is a larger life, not a quieter mind. If Rina has severe depression, the counselor also assesses safety and may combine ACT with behavioral activation.

### Evidence and cautions

ACT and mindfulness-based approaches have growing evidence for various conditions, including anxiety, depression, and chronic pain. DBT has strong evidence for reducing self-harm in borderline personality disorder and is being studied for adolescents. However, these approaches are not replacements for crisis care, trauma treatment, or medication when indicated. Mindfulness can be commercialized and stripped of ethical context. Counselors should consider cultural and religious meanings. Some clients may prefer prayer, movement, or community practices. The counselor should collaborate rather than impose.

### Reflection

Write a thought that often controls your behavior. Practice naming it: "I am noticing the thought that..." Then identify a value the thought has blocked. Take one small action in the direction of that value while allowing the thought to be present. Notice the difference between controlling the thought and choosing your action.

## Chapter 14: Gestalt and Experiential Therapy

Gestalt therapy, associated with Fritz Perls, Laura Perls, and Paul Goodman, emphasizes present-moment awareness, the whole person, and the contact between organism and environment. It is experiential rather than purely explanatory. The counselor does not only talk about experience. The counselor helps the client experience differently in the room. Emotion-focused therapy and other experiential approaches share an interest in accessing and transforming emotion, though they have different histories and methods.

### Awareness and the here and now

Gestalt asks what is happening now: What do you notice in your body? What do you avoid? What do you want? The past is relevant when it is alive in the present. A client may talk about a critical parent while tightening their jaw. The counselor might say, "Notice your jaw as you say that." The goal is not to produce catharsis for its own sake. The goal is to increase awareness so that choice becomes possible.

### Contact and boundary disturbances

Contact is the meeting between self and environment. Healthy contact allows connection and separation. Gestalt describes boundary disturbances such as introjection, projection, retroflection, deflection, and confluence. Introjection is swallowing beliefs without chewing. Projection is attributing one's own experience to others. Retroflection is doing to oneself what one wants to do to others. Deflection is avoiding contact through humor or abstraction. Confluence is losing the boundary between self and other. These concepts should be used as hypotheses, not labels.

### Experiments and chair work

Gestalt uses experiments to expand awareness. A client may speak to an empty chair representing a parent, then switch chairs and answer. The experiment is not a performance. It is a way to access blocked feelings and perspectives. With children, experiments may be playful: drawing the worry, giving it a voice, or using puppets. The counselor must ensure safety. Intense chair work may be inappropriate for clients with psychosis, severe dissociation, or low stability unless carefully adapted.

### Emotion-focused therapy

Emotion-focused therapy, developed by Les Greenberg and others, views emotion as a source of meaning and a target of change. It distinguishes primary adaptive emotions, primary maladaptive emotions, and secondary emotions. A child may feel secondary anger that covers primary sadness. The counselor helps the client access the primary emotion and transform it with a new experience. EFT for individuals and EFT for couples are different approaches; the counselor should not confuse them.

### Clinical example

Fourteen-year-old Diego says he does not care that his father left. He smiles while describing the departure. A Gestalt counselor might notice the smile and ask, "What happens if you let your face say what your words do not?" Diego may become angry, then sad. The counselor stays present and does not rush to explain. Over time, Diego learns that sadness does not destroy him. The experiment is not forced emotion. It is an invitation to notice what is already present.

### Evidence and limits

Experiential therapies have evidence for some conditions, and emotion-focused therapy has a growing research base. Gestalt's historical style, especially confrontational demonstrations, can be misused. Modern relational Gestalt is more collaborative. Counselors should not use experiential techniques to bypass assessment, safety, or cultural humility. A client from a tradition that values emotional restraint may experience chair work as invasive. The counselor should explain the rationale and obtain consent.

### Reflection

Choose a sentence you often say about a difficulty. Say it aloud and notice your body. Then try changing one word or adding a feeling word. Notice what shifts. Experiential work begins with curiosity about lived experience, not with a technique imposed from outside.

## Chapter 15: Family Systems and Postmodern Conversations

Family systems theory views the child not as an isolated problem but as a member of a relational system. Symptoms may serve a function in the family, express loyalty, or reflect stress in the system. This does not mean the family is to blame. It means that change in one part affects other parts. Postmodern approaches, including solution-focused and narrative therapy, shift attention from pathology to language, meaning, and preferred stories. They ask how problems are constructed and what exceptions or alternative stories already exist.

### Systems concepts

A system has boundaries, roles, rules, and feedback loops. Homeostasis is the tendency to maintain stability. A child's symptom may stabilize a family by drawing attention away from marital conflict. Triangulation occurs when a third person is drawn into a two-person tension. A parent may confide in a child about the other parent, placing the child in a loyalty conflict. The counselor maps patterns rather than blaming individuals.

### Structural and strategic family therapy

Structural family therapy, associated with Salvador Minuchin, examines hierarchy, boundaries, and subsystems. A healthy family has clear boundaries and an executive subsystem. Enmeshment means diffuse boundaries; disengagement means rigid boundaries. Strategic family therapy, associated with Jay Haley and others, designs tasks to interrupt problem-maintaining sequences. These approaches can be powerful but require cultural caution. Family structures vary across cultures. A multigenerational household is not automatically enmeshed.

### Solution-focused brief therapy

Solution-focused therapy asks about exceptions, preferred futures, and existing resources. The miracle question invites the client to imagine waking up and the problem being solved: What would be different? Scaling questions ask the client to rate progress and identify what keeps the score from being lower. The approach is brief and future-oriented. It does not ignore problems, but it does not assume that deep historical analysis is always necessary.

### Narrative therapy

Narrative therapy, associated with Michael White and David Epston, separates the person from the problem. A child is not "an anxious child"; the child is a person whom Anxiety visits. Externalizing conversations explore the problem's effects and the client's influence on the problem. Unique outcomes are moments when the problem did not dominate. Re-authoring builds a preferred story. The counselor asks questions that help the client become the author of their life.

### Clinical example

Ten-year-old Emma has stomachaches before school. A systems formulation notes that the parents argue about how to respond. The mother accommodates; the father insists. Emma's symptom draws them into a shared task, temporarily reducing marital tension. The counselor does not tell Emma she is manipulating. The counselor works with the parents to create a consistent plan and addresses the co-parenting conflict. Narrative questions help Emma name Worry and recall times she was brave. Solution-focused scaling tracks small steps.

### Evidence and limits

Family therapy has evidence for various child and adolescent concerns, especially when caregivers are involved. Solution-focused and narrative approaches have growing evidence but may be less studied for severe disorders. A limitation of systems language is that it can minimize abuse or oppression. A child should not be asked to accommodate violence for the sake of system balance. Safety and protection come first. Cultural humility is essential because family roles and communication styles vary.

### Reflection

Draw a simple map of a family or group you know. Mark alliances, conflicts, and roles. Ask what problem might be serving a function. Then ask what would change if the problem improved. Systems thinking is not about blame. It is about seeing the dance.
