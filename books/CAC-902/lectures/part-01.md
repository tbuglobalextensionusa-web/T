# Part I: Foundations and the Psychodynamic Tradition

## Chapter 1: What a Theory Does: Maps, Models, and Evidence

A theory is a map, not the territory. When a counselor meets a child who refuses school, a theory helps the counselor ask useful questions. A behavioral map may ask what happens before and after the refusal. A cognitive map may ask what the child expects will happen at school. A family map may ask who is worried, who is angry, and what changes at home when the child stays home. An attachment map may ask whether the child trusts that adults will respond when distress is high. None of these maps is the whole child. Each map makes some features easier to see and other features easier to miss.

This chapter begins the book with a simple claim: theories are tools for organized curiosity. They are not labels to pin on a young person, and they are not permission to stop listening. A good theory helps a counselor notice patterns, form a tentative explanation, choose an intervention, and check whether the explanation was useful. A bad use of theory happens when the counselor decides in advance what the client must mean. The client then becomes evidence for the theory instead of a person whose experience can correct the theory.

### Learning goals

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to explain the difference between a theory, a model, and a technique; describe why evidence-based practice includes more than a manual; and write a small case formulation that separates observation from interpretation. You should also be able to name one strength and one limit of at least two theoretical orientations. These goals matter because doctoral counseling work requires more than knowing many names. It requires the ability to justify a choice, notice when the choice is not helping, and change course without losing respect for the client.

### Three words that are often confused

A theory is a broad set of ideas about how people change. Psychoanalytic theory, for example, proposes that unconscious conflict and early relationships shape current experience. A model is a more specific picture of a process. A cognitive model of panic may show how a bodily sensation, a catastrophic thought, and a safety behavior keep fear alive. A technique is an action used in session or between sessions. A thought record is a technique. A reflective statement is a technique. A technique can be used well or badly, and the same technique can appear in more than one theory.

Consider a school counselor who asks a student to rate anxiety from zero to ten. The rating itself is not a theory. If the counselor uses the rating to test whether gradual exposure reduces fear, the technique is being guided by a behavioral model. If the counselor uses the rating to help the student notice a felt sense in the body, the same tool may serve an experiential purpose. The meaning of the technique depends on the case formulation and the goal.

### Evidence is a question, not a badge

Students sometimes ask which theory is true. A more practical question is: true for what, for whom, under what conditions, and compared with what? A treatment may have strong evidence for adult panic disorder and weaker evidence for a seven-year-old with separation anxiety and a recent foster placement. A relationship practice may be supported across many studies, while a specific technique may be supported only for a narrow problem. Evidence-based practice asks the counselor to combine research, clinical skill, and the client's culture, preferences, and circumstances.

Imagine two adolescents with depression. One wants a structured plan and likes homework. Another feels controlled by adults and shuts down when given tasks. The same CBT manual may fit the first youth well and damage engagement with the second if used rigidly. The counselor does not abandon evidence. The counselor adapts the pace, language, and goals, and monitors whether the adaptation is working. Flexibility without measurement can become drift. Measurement without flexibility can become a bureaucratic exercise. Doctoral practice holds both.

### A child-adolescent example

Nine-year-old Amina cries when her mother leaves the classroom. The teacher says Amina is manipulative. The mother says Amina is shy. Amina says, "I don't know why, my stomach hurts." A counselor using only a behavioral label might miss grief after a grandmother's death. A counselor using only an attachment label might miss bullying at recess. A counselor using only a medical label might miss the fact that symptoms disappear on weekends. A good formulation begins with observation: when does the stomach pain happen, who responds, what has changed recently, and what does Amina fear will happen?

The counselor can write two columns. In the first column: "Amina cries at classroom door on Monday and Tuesday; mother stays for twenty minutes; pain reported before entry; no pain on Saturday." In the second column: "Possible separation fear; possible avoidance maintained by extra time at home; possible peer problem; possible grief reaction." The second column is hypothesis, not fact. The next step is to test the hypotheses with respectful questions, collateral information, and small changes.

### Common factors and specific ingredients

Research on psychotherapy often distinguishes common factors from specific ingredients. Common factors include alliance, empathy, positive regard, goal agreement, and a credible rationale for treatment. Specific ingredients include exposure, cognitive restructuring, interpretation of transference, or chair work. The debate is not settled by saying that only relationships matter or only techniques matter. A warm relationship without a useful task may not change a phobia. A powerful technique delivered without trust may not be accepted. In child work, the alliance also includes caregivers and systems. A child may trust the counselor while the parent does not trust the plan.

A practical rule is to build the relationship and the rationale together. The counselor might say, "We will try to understand the fear, and we will also practice small steps so your body can learn that school is safe." This sentence offers empathy, a goal, and a method. It also invites correction. If the child says, "It's not fear, it's the noise," the counselor has new information. The theory must move.

### Diagram and reflection

The diagram for this chapter shows assessment feeding formulation, formulation feeding intervention, and feedback returning to assessment. The loop is more important than any single box. A doctoral counselor is not the person who never changes a formulation. A doctoral counselor is the person who changes it for good reasons and documents those reasons.

For reflection, choose one theory you already like. Write what it helps you notice. Then write what it may cause you to overlook. Finally, write one question you could ask a client that would allow the client to disagree with your favorite theory. That question is the beginning of ethical integration.

## Chapter 2: The Helping Relationship and Common Factors

The helping relationship is the working climate in which theory becomes usable. A child does not experience a theory. A child experiences a person who listens, a room that feels safe or unsafe, a parent who is included or excluded, and a plan that makes sense or feels imposed. Common factors are the elements that appear across many therapies: alliance, empathy, collaboration, positive regard, hope, and agreement about goals and tasks. They are not vague kindness. They are observable practices that can be strengthened, repaired, and measured.

### Alliance has three parts

Alliance is often described as bond, goals, and tasks. Bond is the felt sense of trust and respect. Goals are the shared aims of therapy. Tasks are the agreed activities used to reach those aims. A counselor may have a warm bond with a teenager but no agreement about tasks. The teen enjoys talking but refuses to practice a skill. Another counselor may have clear tasks but a weak bond. The teen completes worksheets to please the adult but does not use the skill when distressed. Strong alliance means the three parts are aligned enough for work to continue.

With children, alliance is multiply layered. The child must feel understood. The caregiver must feel respected and see a credible plan. The school may need a role. The counselor must not confuse compliance with alliance. A quiet child who nods may be frightened. A parent who agrees in session may disagree at home because childcare, work, or cultural beliefs make the plan impossible. Alliance work includes asking, "What would make this plan hard to carry out?"

### Empathy is accurate, not merely nice

Empathy means sensing the client's private world as if it were your own, without losing the as-if quality. It is not sympathy, advice, or agreement. A counselor can empathize with a child's anger without agreeing that hitting is acceptable. A counselor can understand a parent's fear without endorsing harsh punishment. Empathy is tested by the client's response. If the counselor says, "You felt left out," and the child says, "No, I felt embarrassed," the correction is data. The counselor should thank the child and adjust.

A simple practice is to reflect content and feeling, then check. "You wanted to sit with your friends, and when they moved away you felt humiliated. Is that close?" The check matters. It gives the client authority over their own experience. In doctoral work, empathy also includes cultural humility. The counselor does not assume that eye contact, emotional expression, or family roles mean the same thing in every community.

### Positive regard and congruence

Unconditional positive regard means valuing the person even when behavior is harmful or symptoms are severe. It does not mean approving of every action. A counselor can say, "I care about what happens to you, and I will not let you hurt yourself or others in this room." Congruence means the counselor is genuine and aware of their own experience, not performing a role so rigidly that the client senses falseness. Congruence does not mean saying every thought. It means that what the counselor says and does is not misleading.

Adolescents are often skilled at detecting incongruence. If a counselor says, "I respect your privacy," but immediately reports every minor statement to a parent without explanation, trust breaks. The counselor should explain confidentiality limits at the start and revisit them when needed. Transparency is a common-factor intervention.

### Rupture and repair

A rupture is a strain or break in the working relationship. It may be obvious, such as a client yelling, or subtle, such as shorter answers and missed appointments. Ruptures are not failures by themselves. They are opportunities to practice repair. Repair begins with noticing, then naming, then listening, then adjusting. "I think I moved too fast last week. You looked away when I suggested the exposure plan. Did I miss something important?" The counselor does not defend the theory first. The counselor protects the relationship enough to think together.

In child therapy, rupture may appear in play. A child who previously built cities may suddenly destroy them when the counselor announces a vacation. The counselor can wonder aloud whether the break feels like abandonment. The interpretation should be tentative and tied to the child's response. Repair may also require caregiver work. If a parent feels blamed, the child's therapy may stall even when the child likes the counselor.

### Measuring the relationship

Feedback tools can help, but they are not a substitute for attention. A short session rating may reveal that a teen felt unheard. A goal-tracking sheet may show that the parent and child want different outcomes. The counselor should ask what the score means. A low score is not an attack. A high score is not proof that therapy is working. The number opens a conversation.

Consider a group for anxious adolescents. Cohesion, safety, and shared goals are common factors in group work. A teen may benefit from hearing that others also fear panic. But if the group becomes a place to compete over who is most impaired, the factor has turned harmful. The counselor must structure the group so that mutual aid is possible. Theory tells the counselor why group work may help; common-factor skills tell the counselor whether this group is helping these members now.

### Real-life application

A school counselor meets with a twelve-year-old who stole a phone. The administrator wants an apology. The parent wants punishment. The child says, "Everyone already thinks I'm bad." A common-factor response does not ignore the theft. It begins with positive regard and accurate empathy: "You expect me to see you as a thief, and that makes it hard to talk." Then the counselor works on goals: repair harm, understand the context, and build a plan to avoid repetition. The tasks may include a behavioral chain analysis, a family conversation, and a restitution plan. The relationship makes the tasks possible.

### Reflection

Think of a time when someone tried to help you but did not understand your goal. What did you do? Did you argue, comply, or withdraw? Clients do the same. The helping relationship is not a soft preface to real therapy. It is the condition in which real therapy becomes possible.

## Chapter 3: Psychoanalysis: Freud and the Unconscious

Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis is one of the most influential and most criticized traditions in counseling. Its influence is not only in technique. It shaped how many people think about childhood, dreams, defense, desire, and the hidden meanings of behavior. A doctoral counselor should know psychoanalysis well enough to use its insights and well enough to recognize its limits. Some Freudian claims are historically important but not supported as universal science. Some clinical ideas, such as defense and transference, remain useful when held as hypotheses rather than dogma.

### The unconscious is not a single thing

Freud used the term unconscious in more than one way. Sometimes it refers to mental processes outside awareness. Sometimes it refers to wishes or conflicts that are kept out of awareness because they are threatening. Modern cognitive science also studies processes outside awareness, but that does not prove every Freudian claim. A counselor can accept that people do not always know why they act without accepting a fixed psychosexual stage theory. Precision matters.

A child who says, "I hate my brother," after a new baby arrives may not be expressing a simple wish. The child may fear losing love, feel guilty for anger, and then act more aggressively to prove that love is still available. A psychodynamic formulation asks what the behavior means in the child's emotional world. It does not stop at the label sibling rivalry.

### Structure and defense

Freud's structural model describes id, ego, and superego. The id represents drive and impulse, the ego mediates reality, and the superego represents internalized standards. These are not brain regions. They are theoretical constructs. Defense mechanisms are ways the ego manages anxiety and conflict. Repression pushes material out of awareness. Denial refuses a painful reality. Projection attributes one's own feeling to another. Reaction formation turns a feeling into its opposite. Sublimation channels impulse into socially valued activity.

In practice, defense language should be used gently. A teenager who jokes about a parent's illness may be using humor to manage terror. The counselor does not say, "You are in denial." The counselor might say, "Jokes seem to help you get through the moment. I wonder if there are moments when the fear comes through anyway." The goal is not to strip defenses away. The goal is to widen the client's capacity to feel and think.

### Psychosexual stages and their limits

Freud proposed that development moves through oral, anal, phallic, latency, and genital stages. Each stage centers on a zone of pleasure and a developmental conflict. Fixation was thought to shape adult personality. This model is historically important, but it should not be used as a literal map of all children. It is culture-bound, gender-bound, and difficult to test in its original form. Contemporary child counseling uses developmental science, attachment research, and trauma research more often than literal psychosexual stages.

Still, the stage theory asks a useful question: how do early experiences of dependency, control, and bodily shame become part of a person's expectations? A child who was shamed harshly during toilet training may not have an anal fixation, but the child may carry shame about bodily control. The counselor can study the meaning without accepting the whole stage theory.

### Free association, dreams, and interpretation

Classical psychoanalysis uses free association: the client says what comes to mind without censoring. The analyst listens for patterns, slips, and connections. Dreams are explored as possible expressions of wish, fear, and conflict. Interpretation offers a hypothesis about unconscious meaning. Transference is the redirection of feelings and expectations from earlier relationships onto the analyst. Countertransference is the therapist's emotional response to the client.

With children, free association may appear in play. A child moves from a dinosaur to a baby to a locked door. The counselor does not force a symbolic dictionary. The counselor follows the affect and the sequence. "The baby keeps getting left outside. I wonder what happens next." If the child corrects the counselor, the correction is part of the work. Interpretation is not a clever statement. It is a shared discovery that the client can use.

### A clinical example

Thirteen-year-old Daniel is referred for oppositional behavior. He mocks the counselor, calls therapy stupid, and arrives late. A purely behavioral plan may miss the meaning of the mockery. Daniel's father left suddenly. Daniel learned that needing someone leads to humiliation. In session, he attacks the counselor before the counselor can disappoint him. The counselor feels useless and angry. That countertransference is information, not a verdict.

A psychodynamic response might be: "It seems important to make sure I don't get too important to you. If I matter, then I could let you down." Daniel may reject the statement. The counselor waits. Over time, Daniel tests whether the counselor survives his anger without retaliation. The therapy does not ignore behavior limits. It links the limit to the relationship: "I won't let you break the lamp, and I'm still here to understand what just happened."

### Evidence and criticism

Psychoanalytic therapy has a research base, especially for certain adult conditions and for short-term psychodynamic approaches. The evidence is not uniform across all problems or all versions of the theory. Critics note that classical concepts can be unfalsifiable, that treatment can be long and costly, and that early formulations often reflected the biases of their time. A counselor should not present psychoanalysis as the only deep therapy. Cognitive, behavioral, systemic, and humanistic therapies can also address meaning and pattern.

For children and adolescents, psychodynamic work is often adapted. Play therapy, time-limited psychodynamic therapy, and mentalization-based approaches may be more practical than classical analysis. The counselor must consider development, family context, school demands, and safety. A long interpretive silence may be experienced by a child as abandonment rather than neutrality.

### Reflection

Choose a behavior that puzzles you in a young client. Write one behavioral hypothesis, one cognitive hypothesis, and one psychodynamic hypothesis. Do not choose a winner yet. Ask what information would help you tell them apart. Psychoanalysis earns its place when it generates questions that can be checked in the relationship and in the client's life.

## Chapter 4: Object Relations and Attachment-Informed Therapy

Object relations theory shifts attention from drive to relationship. The word object is historical and can sound cold. In this tradition, an object is usually a person, or an internal image of a person, toward whom feelings are directed. The central question is not only what drive seeks discharge, but how early relationships become internal patterns that shape later experience. A child does not only learn that a caregiver feeds them. The child learns whether the world is responsive, whether distress can be shared, and whether the self is worthy of care.

### Internal working models

Attachment research, associated with John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, describes internal working models of self and other. A child who experiences consistent responsiveness may develop an expectation that help is available. A child who experiences unpredictable care may become vigilant and preoccupied. A child who experiences rejection of need may minimize attachment signals. These patterns are not destiny. They are adaptations to a caregiving environment. A counselor should not use attachment language to blame a child or a parent. The pattern made sense in the context in which it developed.

Object relations theorists such as Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, and Ronald Fairbairn described related ideas in different ways. Klein emphasized early fantasies and the integration of love and hate. Winnicott emphasized the holding environment, the good-enough mother, and the true self. Fairbairn emphasized that the child seeks relationship, not merely pleasure. These theories are not identical, and they should not be collapsed into one simple story.

### Splitting and integration

Splitting is the separation of experience into all-good and all-bad parts. A young child may do this developmentally. Under stress, older clients may also experience people as either perfect or worthless. A teenager with intense relationship instability may idealize a counselor after one session and devalue the counselor after a boundary is set. The counselor should not take either position as the whole truth. The task is to help the client hold mixed feelings: "I am disappointed in you, and you have also helped me."

In child therapy, splitting may appear in play. The good queen is protected; the bad witch is destroyed. The counselor does not interpret too quickly. The play may be practicing integration. Over time, the story may allow a character to be both angry and loved. That narrative change can be a clinical marker.

### The holding environment

Winnicott's holding environment is not only physical holding. It is the reliable provision of care that allows the infant to develop without constant impingement. In therapy, the frame can function as a holding environment: consistent time, clear boundaries, and a therapist who survives the client's affect without retaliation. For a child, the holding environment may include the room, the toys, the routine, and the caregiver's participation. A chaotic waiting room or frequent cancellations can undo the sense of safety.

A good-enough therapist is not perfect. The therapist makes small mistakes and repairs them. The repair teaches that relationship can survive imperfection. A child who has experienced unpredictable care may test the frame. The counselor responds with calm consistency rather than punishment. "We have ten minutes left. I know it is hard to stop. We will continue next week." The boundary is part of care.

### Transitional phenomena

Winnicott described transitional objects, such as a blanket or teddy, as existing in an intermediate area between inner and outer reality. The object is not merely a thing. It supports the child's move from dependence toward separateness. In therapy, a drawing, a stone, or a small toy may serve a transitional function. The counselor should respect the meaning without turning the object into a magical cure. A child may keep a drawing from session to remember that the counselor exists between meetings.

Adolescents may use music, a hoodie, or a phone wallpaper as transitional supports. The counselor can ask what the object helps them remember. The question links the object to internalization. Over time, the client may no longer need the external object because the soothing function has become internal.

### Attachment-informed assessment

An attachment-informed assessment asks how the child seeks comfort, how the caregiver responds, and what happens when distress is high. It does not assign a label after one observation. A child who does not seek comfort may have learned that seeking is unsafe. A caregiver who appears intrusive may be responding to a child's medical trauma. The counselor looks for cycles. Child distress leads to caregiver anxiety; caregiver anxiety leads to overcontrol; overcontrol leads to child protest; protest confirms caregiver fear. The cycle is the target, not the blame.

### Clinical application

Seven-year-old Leo was adopted after several foster moves. He controls play, hides food, and becomes furious when the counselor ends a session. A behavioral plan alone may increase his sense of threat. An attachment-informed approach combines predictability, caregiver coaching, and play. The counselor uses the same opening ritual each week. The caregiver is helped to respond to Leo's fear rather than only his control. When Leo hides food, the caregiver might say, "You want to make sure there is enough. We keep food in the kitchen, and I will make sure you eat." The limit is clear; the meaning is recognized.

The counselor also watches their own countertransference. Leo may make the counselor feel incompetent or rejected. The counselor does not withdraw. The feeling is discussed in supervision and used to understand Leo's expectation that adults leave when he is difficult. Therapy becomes a place where difficulty does not end relationship.

### Limits and integration

Attachment language can be misused. A counselor should not diagnose attachment disorders loosely or assume that all behavior problems are attachment problems. Autism, ADHD, trauma, language delay, and contextual stress can look similar in some settings. Assessment must be broad. Object relations concepts can also become vague if every behavior is interpreted as an internal object relation. The counselor should tie interpretations to observable patterns and client feedback.

Integration is common. A counselor may use attachment-informed formulation, behavioral caregiver coaching, and cognitive work with an adolescent. The theory provides a map of relational expectation; the techniques provide steps. The relationship provides the experience that can update the map.

### Reflection

Recall a time when you expected someone to reject you. How did you protect yourself? Did you demand reassurance, withdraw, or test the person? Clients protect themselves in similar ways. The therapeutic task is not to remove protection by force. It is to make protection less necessary.

## Chapter 5: Adler and Sullivan: Belonging, Goals, and Interpersonal Life

Alfred Adler and Harry Stack Sullivan are often grouped with psychodynamic thinkers, but both moved theory toward social life. Adler emphasized belonging, inferiority, goals, and social interest. Sullivan emphasized interpersonal relationships and the self-system that develops to manage anxiety. For child and adolescent counseling, these traditions are especially useful because young people are constantly negotiating belonging in families, peer groups, schools, and online communities.

### Adler: inferiority and striving

Adler proposed that feelings of inferiority are a normal part of human life. A child is small, dependent, and less skilled than adults. The question is not whether inferiority exists, but how the child responds. Some children strive toward competence and contribution. Others develop a superiority pose, avoidance, or excessive approval-seeking. The counselor asks what the child is moving toward, away from, or against. The behavior is purposeful, even when the purpose is outside awareness.

A ten-year-old who disrupts class may be seeking significance. If the child cannot feel useful through contribution, the child may feel visible through disruption. The counselor does not excuse harm. The counselor asks what goal the behavior serves and how the child could meet the need differently. Encouragement is central. Encouragement is not empty praise. It notices effort, contribution, and possibility.

### Birth order and family constellation

Adler gave attention to birth order and family atmosphere. Firstborn, middle, youngest, and only children may experience different positions, but birth order is not destiny. A child who is oldest but has a chronic illness may not experience the usual role. A child who is youngest in a large extended family may have many caregivers. The counselor studies the subjective family constellation: how the child experiences their place. Questions include: Who is expected to succeed? Who is protected? Who is blamed? Who is compared with whom?

In doctoral assessment, family constellation can be mapped with a genogram and a simple question: "What is it like to be you in this family?" The answer may reveal a role such as the responsible one, the funny one, or the problem. Therapy can help the child expand the role.

### Social interest and encouragement

Social interest, or Gemeinschaftsgefühl, refers to a sense of connection and contribution to the common good. Adler saw mental health not as isolated self-esteem but as the courage to participate. A child who feels discouraged may withdraw or dominate. Encouragement helps the child experience capability and belonging. The counselor might involve the child in a real contribution: helping set up the playroom, teaching the counselor a game, or planning a family task. The contribution must be genuine, not a trick to produce compliance.

### Sullivan: anxiety and the self-system

Sullivan argued that personality is shaped in interpersonal situations. The self-system develops to manage anxiety, especially anxiety transmitted by important others. A child learns which feelings are acceptable and which lead to disapproval. Over time, the child may develop a persona that protects against anxiety but limits authenticity. Sullivan's concepts of prototaxic, parataxic, and syntaxic experience describe early undifferentiated experience, distorted interpersonal perception, and shared logical understanding. The counselor need not use the terms heavily, but the idea is useful: some interpersonal reactions are based on old, unexamined patterns.

Parataxic distortion is similar to transference but framed interpersonally. A teenager may assume a counselor will criticize them because a previous adult did. The counselor can notice the pattern and invite a new experience. "You expected me to be angry. What do you notice now?" The question is not a debate. It is an invitation to update the interpersonal map.

### Clinical example

Fifteen-year-old Maya is a high achiever who panics before exams. Adlerian formulation might note that Maya's significance depends on being the successful child. A younger brother's illness has shifted family attention. Maya's striving protects against feeling forgotten. Sullivan's formulation might note that Maya learned anxiety when a parent's approval felt conditional. The counselor combines encouragement, family conversation, and cognitive-behavioral exam strategies. The goal is not only lower panic. The goal is a wider base of belonging that does not depend on perfect performance.

The counselor asks, "If you got a B, what would it mean about you?" Maya says, "That I'm not needed." The sentence reveals the interpersonal meaning of achievement. Therapy can test the belief through family dialogue and small experiments. Maya might share a struggle with a parent and observe the response. The experiment is behavioral, but the target is belonging.

### Evidence and limits

Adlerian and interpersonal ideas influence many modern approaches, including family therapy, school consultation, and brief psychodynamic work. The research base is not as large as for CBT for some disorders, but the concepts are clinically generative. A limitation is that purpose can be overread. Not every behavior is a hidden goal. Sleep loss, hunger, neurodevelopmental differences, and unsafe environments matter. The counselor should not turn social context into a personal strategy.

### Reflection

Write about a time when you felt you did not belong. What did you do to regain a place? Did you achieve, withdraw, rebel, or caretake? Young clients are often doing the same. Adler and Sullivan remind us that symptoms can be attempts to solve the problem of belonging.
