# Glossary

**Acceptance:** Willingness to experience thoughts, feelings, and sensations without unnecessary struggle, especially when struggle blocks valued action.

**Alliance:** The working relationship in therapy, including bond, agreement about goals, and agreement about tasks.

**Archetype:** In Jungian theory, a proposed universal pattern of image or behavior; used clinically as metaphor rather than settled science.

**Attachment:** An enduring emotional bond that shapes expectations about safety, comfort, and availability of care.

**Automatic thought:** A quick, often unnoticed interpretation of a situation that influences emotion and behavior.

**Basic anxiety:** Horney's term for the feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world.

**Behavioral activation:** A treatment that increases contact with rewarding and meaningful activities to reduce depression.

**Case formulation:** A testable hypothesis that links assessment information to a treatment plan.

**Cognitive defusion:** Noticing thoughts as mental events rather than literal truths that must control action.

**Cognitive restructuring:** Examining and revising unhelpful interpretations through evidence and alternative perspectives.

**Common factors:** Elements shared across therapies, such as alliance, empathy, hope, and goal agreement.

**Congruence:** Genuineness or transparency in the therapist, within professional limits.

**Countertransference:** The therapist's emotional reactions to the client, used as information rather than acted out.

**Defense mechanism:** A psychological strategy that manages anxiety or conflict, often outside awareness.

**Dereflection:** A logotherapy technique that shifts attention from excessive self-focus toward meaningful action.

**Dialectics:** Holding opposites together, especially acceptance and change, as in DBT.

**Empathy:** Accurate understanding of the client's inner frame of reference, communicated and checked.

**Exposure:** Planned approach to feared stimuli so that new safety learning can occur.

**Externalization:** Separating the person from the problem, as in narrative therapy.

**Functional assessment:** Analysis of antecedents, behavior, and consequences to identify what maintains a behavior.

**Gestalt:** An experiential approach emphasizing present awareness, contact, and the whole person.

**Holding environment:** A reliable relational frame that supports development and repair, from Winnicott.

**Individuation:** In Jungian theory, the process of becoming a more whole and integrated person.

**Internal working model:** An expectation about self and others formed in early caregiving relationships.

**Logotherapy:** Frankl's approach centered on the will to meaning.

**Mentalization:** The capacity to understand behavior in terms of mental states such as feelings, beliefs, and intentions.

**Mindfulness:** Paying attention to present experience with curiosity and reduced reactivity.

**Negative reinforcement:** Increasing a behavior because it removes an aversive condition.

**Object relations:** Psychodynamic theories emphasizing internalized relationship patterns.

**Parataxic distortion:** Sullivan's term for distorted interpersonal perception based on earlier patterns.

**Persona:** In Jungian theory, the social mask presented to the world.

**Positive reinforcement:** Increasing a behavior because it adds a valued consequence.

**Projective identification:** A psychodynamic process in which a person induces feelings in another that match disowned experience.

**Psychological flexibility:** The ability to stay present and choose values-guided action even when difficult experiences are present.

**Rupture and repair:** A strain in the therapeutic relationship followed by acknowledgment, listening, and adjustment.

**Self-efficacy:** Belief in one's capability to organize and execute actions needed for a situation.

**Shadow:** In Jungian theory, qualities excluded from the conscious self-image.

**Social interest:** Adler's term for connection and contribution to the common good.

**Splitting:** Experiencing self or others as all-good or all-bad rather than integrated.

**Technical eclecticism:** Choosing techniques from different approaches based on evidence and fit, without necessarily merging theories.

**Theoretical integration:** Combining theories into a coherent framework.

**Transference:** Redirecting feelings and expectations from earlier relationships onto the therapist.

**Transitional object:** An object that supports a child's move from dependence toward separateness.

**Unconditional positive regard:** Valuing the client without making worth conditional on behavior.

**Working through:** Repeatedly examining and experiencing a pattern until it becomes more flexible.

**Zone of proximal development:** The gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with support; useful for developmental adaptation.
