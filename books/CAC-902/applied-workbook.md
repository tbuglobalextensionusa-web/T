# Applied Workbook: Theory-to-Practice Exercises

This workbook turns each chapter into a small professional task. Use fictional or de-identified cases. Do not practice intense techniques on real clients without training, consent, and supervision. The goal is not to prove a theory right. The goal is to practice reasoning, humility, and evidence-informed choice.

## Exercise 1: Theory as a map
Choose one child behavior, such as school refusal. Write three hypotheses from three different theories. For each, list one observation that would support it and one observation that would challenge it. End with a question you would ask the child to check your map.

## Exercise 2: Alliance audit
Recall a helping conversation. Rate bond, goal agreement, and task agreement from zero to ten. Identify the weakest part. Write one repair sentence you could use, such as, "I think I moved too fast. What did I miss?"

## Exercise 3: Defense and meaning
Write a behavior that puzzles you. Describe it without interpretation. Then write a possible defense or protective function. Finally, write a gentle, tentative statement that invites the client to confirm or correct the hypothesis.

## Exercise 4: Attachment cycle map
Draw a cycle: child distress, caregiver response, child expectation, future behavior. Mark where a new response could interrupt the cycle. Write one caregiver coaching sentence that validates the child's feeling while keeping a limit.

## Exercise 5: Belonging strategy
Identify a child's apparent goal: attention, power, revenge, avoidance, or contribution. Write one encouragement statement that is specific and genuine. Avoid empty praise. Explain how the statement supports social interest.

## Exercise 6: Protective moves
Write a case where a client moves toward, against, or away from people. Identify the anxiety underneath. Write an intervention that increases safety so the client can choose more flexibly.

## Exercise 7: Symbolic work with consent
Choose a client image or metaphor. Write three questions that follow the client's meaning rather than imposing an interpretation. Include one question that asks what the image protects and one that asks what it invites.

## Exercise 8: Core conditions script
Write a short dialogue in which a counselor demonstrates congruence, positive regard, and empathy with a child who failed a test. Include a check for accuracy. Then write what the counselor should not say.

## Exercise 9: Meaning in a constrained life
Write a case where a client cannot change a major circumstance. Identify one response that remains possible. Write a values question that does not minimize the injustice. Explain how you would assess safety.

## Exercise 10: Functional behavior plan
Choose a behavior. Write antecedents, behavior, consequences, and a hypothesis about function. Design a replacement behavior and a reinforcement plan. State how you would know the plan is working.

## Exercise 11: Thought record and experiment
Write a situation, automatic thought, emotion, and behavior. Generate one alternative interpretation. Design a small behavioral experiment. State what result would make you revise the formulation.

## Exercise 12: Self-efficacy ladder
Choose a goal a teen avoids. Build a mastery ladder with five steps. Identify a coping model, a source of encouragement, and a way to reinterpret arousal. Write the first step the teen could take this week.

## Exercise 13: Values and defusion
Write a controlling thought. Practice naming it as a thought. Identify a value it blocks. Write one committed action that can be taken while the thought is present. Explain why the goal is not thought elimination.

## Exercise 14: Awareness experiment
Write a sentence a client says while showing a body cue. Design a gentle present-moment experiment. Include a consent check and a stop rule. Explain how you would ground the client afterward.

## Exercise 15: Externalizing conversation
Take a problem label, such as anxiety, and externalize it. Write five questions: naming, effects, exceptions, influence, and preferred story. Avoid blaming the child.

## Exercise 16: Developmental adaptation
Choose an adult technique, such as cognitive restructuring. Adapt it for a seven-year-old using play or art. Write the materials, the steps, and the caregiver role. State what would make you simplify further.

## Exercise 17: Cultural formulation
Write five questions for a family from a different cultural background. Include meaning, identity, supports, power, and barriers. Write one assumption you must not make.

## Exercise 18: Four P's formulation
Write predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, and protective factors for a case. Then write one testable prediction. State what would make you revise the formulation.

## Exercise 19: Integration plan
Choose a primary theory and two supplements for a case. Justify each choice with evidence, formulation, and client preference. Write a measurement plan and a criterion for changing course.

## Exercise 20: Capstone orientation statement
Write a one-page statement of your theoretical orientation. Include your view of change, the relationship, evidence, culture, and uncertainty. End with three learning goals for supervision.

## Portfolio guidance
Collect your best exercises into a portfolio. For each, include the task, your response, feedback received, and a revision. A doctoral portfolio should show growth in reasoning, not just polished final answers. Keep client information de-identified and follow your program's confidentiality rules.
