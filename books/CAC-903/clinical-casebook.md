# Clinical Casebook: Assessment Reasoning in Practice

The following fictional cases are teaching examples. They are not diagnostic protocols and do not replace supervision. Each case asks you to practice the chapter's reasoning: clarify the question, choose methods, weigh evidence, and design a helpful next step.

## Case 1: The referral that was too small
A teacher refers eight-year-old Musa for "ADHD testing." The teacher reports that Musa leaves his seat, interrupts, and fails to finish work. The parent reports that Musa is calm at home but forgets chores. The initial referral assumes one disorder. The assessment team rewrites the question: "What explains Musa's classroom inattention and activity, and what supports are needed?" The team collects teacher and parent ratings, classroom observation, sleep history, hearing screening, and academic review. Observation shows that Musa is most active during long written tasks and calmer during hands-on activities. Sleep history reveals late bedtimes. The formulation includes possible attention difficulties, sleep loss, and task mismatch. The team recommends sleep routine support, movement breaks, and further evaluation if impairment persists. The case teaches that a referral label is a starting point, not a conclusion.

## Case 2: Permission without understanding
A clinic obtains a signed consent form from a mother before assessing ten-year-old Ada. The counselor begins testing without explaining the process to Ada. Ada becomes quiet and refuses items. The supervisor pauses the session and asks what Ada understood. Ada says she thought she was being punished. The counselor provides assent in simple language, uses a visual schedule, and offers a break. Ada participates more fully. The case teaches that legal permission is not enough. A child needs developmentally appropriate understanding and a sense of safety. Assent is an ongoing process, not a one-time signature.

## Case 3: The interpreter who was not neutral
A family speaks a language the counselor does not know. The clinic asks the adolescent daughter to interpret for her mother. During the interview, the daughter softens the mother's concerns and omits family conflict. The counselor notices the mismatch and arranges a qualified interpreter. The mother then describes grief and financial stress. The case teaches that using a child as an interpreter can reverse roles and distort data. Language access is an ethical and validity issue. The counselor should protect the child from adult burdens and use qualified support.

## Case 4: Laziness or reading failure?
A teacher describes nine-year-old Grace as lazy. Grace refuses reading tasks and says she hates school. The counselor avoids the label and maps the construct. Observation shows that Grace starts nonreading tasks but avoids decoding. Achievement assessment shows weak word reading. The formulation is reading difficulty with secondary avoidance. The plan includes structured reading intervention, accessible text, and praise for effort. The case teaches that behavior labels often hide skill deficits. Assessment should translate labels into observable processes.

## Case 5: The score that frightened a parent
A parent hears that a child's standard score is 92 and concludes the child is "below normal." The counselor explains the average range, confidence interval, and functional meaning. The counselor also describes strengths in verbal reasoning and practical problem-solving. The parent's anxiety decreases. The case teaches that scores require translation. A number without context can harm a family's expectations. The counselor should explain what the score supports and what it does not support.

## Case 6: Weekly scores that jumped around
A teen's depression scores move from 18 to 14 to 19 to 15 over four weeks. The counselor does not declare failure. The counselor reviews measurement error, life events, and functioning. The teen reports improved sleep but increased family conflict. The plan adds family communication work. The case teaches that fluctuation is not automatically change. Trends, context, and reliable change matter more than single points.

## Case 7: A valid test used for the wrong question
A school uses a depression scale to decide which students need career counseling. The counselor objects that the tool was not designed for that decision. The team instead uses interest inventories, academic records, and student interviews. The case teaches that validity is purpose-specific. A measure can be strong for one question and weak for another. The counselor should match method to decision.

## Case 8: Overrepresentation in discipline referrals
A district notices that one group of students is referred more often for emotional disturbance. The assessment team reviews language access, trauma exposure, instructional quality, and bias in referral language. They add bilingual interviews and classroom observation. Referrals become more accurate. The case teaches that fairness is a pathway issue. A test may not be the only source of bias. The counselor should examine the whole decision system.

## Case 9: The adolescent who said everything was fine
Fourteen-year-old Tolu answers "fine" to every question. The counselor notices tension and avoids pressure. The counselor offers a drawing of a typical school day. Tolu draws a crowded hallway and labels it "too much." The interview reveals sensory overload and bullying. The case teaches that flexible methods can open communication. The counselor should not force disclosure but can create safer ways to share.

## Case 10: Hitting that produced attention
A child hits peers during recess. Observation shows that hitting occurs after exclusion and ends when an adult intervenes. The team hypothesizes attention and escape from social confusion. The plan teaches help-seeking and peer-entry skills. Adults respond calmly and reinforce appropriate requests. The case teaches that behavior has function. Intervention should match the maintaining consequence, not only the topography.

## Case 11: Not listening or not remembering?
A child fails to follow multi-step instructions. Cognitive assessment shows weak working memory. The classroom uses long verbal directions. The plan includes visual steps, chunked instructions, and check-backs. The case teaches that cognitive assessment should identify demands and supports. A behavior may reflect a mismatch between task and capacity.

## Case 12: Low score after migration
A child recently migrated and scores low on a verbal test. Adaptive reports show strong daily skills in the home language. The counselor uses nonverbal tasks, dynamic assessment, and interpreter-supported interview. The report avoids a global deficit label. The case teaches that language exposure can depress scores. Intellectual assessment must be interpreted with opportunity and context.

## Case 13: The teen who hid depression
A teen reports high depression; the parent reports low depression. The interview reveals private hopelessness and masking at home. The counselor takes the teen's report seriously and assesses safety. The case teaches that informant disagreement is meaningful. Inner experience may be visible only to the child. The counselor should not average away distress.

## Case 14: The drawing that was not proof
A child draws a dark house after hospitalization. A trainee suggests the drawing proves trauma. The supervisor cautions against overinterpretation. The counselor asks the child about the drawing and follows play themes over time. The child introduces a helper and a key. The case teaches that expressive methods generate hypotheses. They do not replace corroborated evidence.

## Case 15: The vivid prediction
A counselor predicts a teen will drop out because of a dramatic interview. A structured review shows moderate risk, not high risk. The counselor adjusts the plan to monitoring and support. The case teaches that vivid details can distort judgment. Structured rules and feedback improve calibration.

## Case 16: The positive screen that was not a diagnosis
A school depression screen identifies many students. The counselor does not diagnose all. Follow-up interviews assess impairment, safety, and context. Some students need support; others had temporary stress. The case teaches that screening is a first step. Base rates and follow-up protect against overidentification.

## Case 17: Monitoring that changed the plan
A teen's weekly scores improve, then rise after a family conflict. The counselor reviews the data with the teen and adds family communication work. The case teaches that monitoring is a conversation. Data should guide adjustment, not punish the client.

## Case 18: The mood app that excluded a family
A clinic recommends a mood app, but a family lacks a smartphone. The counselor offers paper logs and phone check-ins. The case teaches that digital methods require equity planning. Technology should not become a barrier to care.

## Case 19: The report that blamed the child
A report describes a child as manipulative. The supervisor asks for a functional formulation. The revised report describes crying that delays difficult tasks and is reinforced by escape. The plan becomes clearer. The case teaches that language shapes intervention. Blame labels close thinking; functional descriptions open options.

## Case 20: The integrated formulation
A nine-year-old refuses school after grief and bullying. Assessment includes child and caregiver interviews, teacher ratings, arrival observation, and record review. The formulation links separation fear, avoidance, and accommodation. The plan includes gradual exposure, caregiver coaching, school safety, and grief support. Monitoring tracks attendance and distress. The case teaches that integrated assessment connects evidence to action. The child is not a score. The child is a person with a future.
