# Applied Developmental Workbook

The exercises below extend the course lectures. They are practice in reasoning, not tests of a real child and not instructions to conduct research without approval. Use fictional information or properly de-identified material only within an authorized learning setting. Do not place a child’s identifying information in a public seminar document.

## Exercise 1: Build a timeline before choosing a label

A school reports that a nine-year-old has become inattentive. Create a timeline with four columns: date or period, observed behavior, setting and demands, and changes in relationships or health. Include at least one period before the concern began. Add the child’s account in a separate column so it is not confused with adult interpretation.

Mark each statement as an observation, a report from another person, or a hypothesis. “Appears anxious” is an interpretation unless the observable signs are specified. “Asked to leave the room three times during written work” is more precise. Do not assume that a behavior beginning after a school transition was caused only by that transition. The timeline organizes questions; it does not prove causation.

For review, ask a colleague to identify the strongest description and the weakest inference. Discuss what additional information would change your formulation. A good answer includes periods of successful functioning, not only difficulty. End with two competing explanations and a proportionate next step for each.

## Exercise 2: Write an assent conversation

Imagine a study inviting ten-year-olds to complete a weekly feelings questionnaire and discuss a school coping program. Write an explanation of no more than 250 words. Include what will happen, how long it takes, who will see the information, the limits of privacy, and the right to decline or stop. Avoid saying that the program will certainly help.

Now write three questions to check understanding without making the child repeat a memorized script. For example: “What could you do if you did not want to answer a question?” Record what the study team would do if the child says yes but later appears distressed. Explain how permission from a legally authorized representative differs from the child’s assent.

Review the conversation for pressure. Would a child believe that refusal affects grades, counseling access, or a teacher’s opinion? Propose a recruitment process that reduces that pressure. Check local law and ethics requirements rather than treating this exercise as legal authorization.

## Exercise 3: Separate two questions in repeated data

Use a fictional dataset with five adolescents, four weekly observations of sleep duration, and four weekly distress ratings. You do not need to calculate a model. First describe how you would examine whether adolescents who generally sleep more differ in average distress. Then describe how you would examine whether a particular adolescent reports more distress than usual in weeks with less sleep than usual.

Create two small graphs. One compares person averages. The other centers each adolescent around their own average and shows weekly deviations. Explain why the graphs answer different questions. Identify a factor, such as examination pressure, that could influence both sleep and distress.

For discussion, write one sentence that overgeneralizes from the between-person pattern to an individual intervention. Rewrite it as a bounded statement. Then identify what an intervention trial or carefully monitored individual plan could add. The goal is not to reject group research; it is to use it at the right level.

## Exercise 4: Compare explanations without collecting every technique

Choose a fictional child who avoids group work. Write four brief formulations: cognitive, learning-based, relational, and ecological. Each must identify a mechanism, a possible observation, and an intervention implication. Do not use a theorist’s name as a substitute for explanation.

Now add information that the child reports being mocked during presentations. Which formulation changes? Which environmental action becomes necessary before expecting the child to practice participation? Identify one technique that would be inappropriate if used alone.

Review your final plan for coherence. It should explain why the chosen actions belong together and which outcome would show that they are helping. Include the child’s sense of safety as well as visible participation. A quiet child is not necessarily a successfully supported child.

## Exercise 5: Adapt a task without changing the child’s worth

A seven-year-old is asked to explain a recent conflict with a friend. The adult’s original question is long and contains several assumptions. Rewrite the invitation using short sentences and one idea at a time. Offer a drawing or sequence cards as an optional communication aid.

List what the adapted method can reveal and what it cannot establish. A drawing may support conversation, but it does not provide a fixed symbolic code for the child’s experience. Write two neutral follow-up questions and two questions that would be too leading. Explain the difference.

Finally, describe how you would involve a caregiver without requiring the child’s private account to be repeated in full. Identify information that is necessary for support and information that can remain private under applicable rules.

## Exercise 6: Challenge a biological certainty claim

An advertisement says that a child’s attention problem can be identified from one brain measure and corrected by a proprietary training package. Write a list of questions about the measure’s validity, the population studied, the comparison condition, the size and uncertainty of effects, and possible conflicts of interest.

Separate three claims: the measure is associated with attention in a research sample; the measure can diagnose an individual; the training causes meaningful improvement in daily life. Explain why evidence for the first does not automatically establish the other two.

Write a short response to a caregiver who is interested in the package. Be respectful and avoid ridicule. Acknowledge the desire for help, explain the uncertainty, and suggest a proportionate assessment pathway. Identify when medical or specialist consultation would be appropriate.

## Exercise 7: Design support for a real decision moment

A fifteen-year-old understands a safety rule but sometimes breaks it when peers are watching. Design a plan that addresses both the decision moment and the surrounding conditions. Include a pause cue, a practicable exit strategy, a trusted adult, and a change in the environment where feasible.

Do not assume that role-play in a quiet office will transfer automatically. Specify where and how the young person can practice, what might make practice embarrassing, and how the plan can preserve dignity. Ask the young person to identify the part that seems least realistic.

Choose two indicators of progress. One may concern behavior, but the other should concern safety, agency, or the ability to seek support. Set a review date and a rule for revising the plan. Avoid explaining the entire concern with a claim about an immature brain.

## Exercise 8: Test a behavioral hypothesis ethically

A child leaves the table when a difficult task begins. Adults respond by removing the task. Write an operational definition of the behavior and identify setting events that might affect it. Generate an escape hypothesis and a communication hypothesis; explain how they may overlap.

Design a support that teaches a respectful help request and makes the task more accessible. Specify what adults will do when the request is used. Include a baseline, a review period, and a measure of task participation. Do not use deprivation, humiliation, or escalating punishment as a convenience.

Now imagine that the behavior decreases but the child remains distressed. Explain why the plan may still be incomplete. Review the child’s experience, the task’s difficulty, and whether the behavior was a reasonable response to an inaccessible demand. Ethical evaluation includes more than compliance.

## Exercise 9: Analyze hidden task demands

Choose a school assignment that requires planning over several days. Break it into steps and identify the language, memory, organization, and emotional demands of each step. Mark where a child could understand the goal but struggle with the route.

Design a visual support that preserves the learning objective. Include a way for the child to choose among acceptable approaches and a plan for reducing assistance. Do not turn the support into permanent adult completion of the task.

Write two outcome measures: successful task progress and the child’s ability to use the planning process. Explain how you would distinguish improved access from simply making the assignment less demanding. Include a review of whether the support is acceptable in the actual classroom.

## Exercise 10: Audit a score-based decision

A school proposes a fixed placement based on one group-administered score. Create an audit checklist covering the referral question, test norms, language, administration, measurement error, daily functioning, and the consequences of placement. Identify which missing information would prevent a confident decision.

Write a caregiver explanation that does not call the score the child’s identity. Include strengths, needs, uncertainty, and a review plan. Then write a team recommendation that identifies what support should be provided while further information is gathered.

Discuss why rejecting all standardized measures and accepting every score without question are both poor responses. The goal is evidence that fits the decision, not loyalty to or rejection of testing in general.

## Exercise 11: Protect an account from suggestion

Review a fictional conversation in which an adult repeatedly asks a child whether a named person caused a particular event. Mark the adult’s assumptions and the child’s spontaneous statements. Rewrite the opening using a neutral invitation and a calm response to silence.

Separate the counselor’s responsibilities from those of an investigative interviewer. Identify the immediate safety actions that cannot wait for a complete narrative. Write a documentation note using exact quotation, observed behavior, and interpretation in separate sections.

Explain why a detailed account is not automatically complete and a sparse account is not automatically false. Avoid using this exercise to rehearse a real allegation outside authorized professional channels. The purpose is careful communication and role clarity.

## Exercise 12: Plan co-regulation for two different children

One child becomes quiet and withdraws when distressed; another becomes loud and active. Design an initial response for each without assuming that the outward behavior reveals the whole emotional state. Include adult tone, space, choices, communication supports, and safety boundaries.

Identify a common regulation technique that might be uncomfortable for one child. Offer an alternative and a way to review what helped. Do not require breathing, eye contact, or touch as proof of cooperation.

Write a short caregiver script that validates emotion and sets a boundary. Then describe how the adult can reflect afterward on their own contribution to escalation or recovery. The plan should increase usable support rather than demand a particular performance of calm.

## Exercise 13: Identify conditions of worth

A child says, “I am only the good one when I do not need help.” Map possible sources of this belief across family expectations, school rewards, peer comparison, and the child’s own interpretation. Avoid assuming that any one source is responsible.

Design a response that changes the child’s reflective resources and at least one adult response. Include honest feedback that does not depend on flawless performance. Identify an activity in which the child can experience belonging without proving competence.

Choose an outcome that reflects flexibility rather than higher achievement alone. Explain how the child’s account will be included. Review whether the intervention unintentionally turns self-acceptance into another performance target.

## Exercise 14: Map peer resources and risks

Create a peer-ecology map for a fictional adolescent. Include close friends, group status, trusted adults, digital spaces, and places where the young person feels safe. Mark supportive connections and pressures without treating every online relationship as either dangerous or therapeutic.

The young person reports exclusion but does not want immediate confrontation. Develop a plan that protects safety, preserves supportive relationships, and changes the school environment. Identify why removing the device or forcing a joint meeting could be harmful.

Define success from both the young person’s perspective and the school’s safeguarding responsibilities. Explain how disagreement will be handled and when urgent action overrides a preference for delay. The map should guide collaborative reasoning, not surveillance.

## Exercise 15: Make a helping pathway safe

A class repeatedly mocks a student’s language. Design a bystander-support plan with several routes: seek an adult, support the targeted student afterward, interrupt only when safe, and report through an accessible channel. Do not require a child to confront a dangerous group alone.

Specify what adults must do when harm is reported. Include protection from retaliation, a review of group patterns, and support for the targeted student that does not require public explanation of identity. Identify why an assembly alone may be insufficient.

Write indicators for reduced harm and increased participation. Include a check for unintended consequences, such as students being punished for reporting. A school’s response is part of the intervention, not merely the background.

## Exercise 16: Redefine resilience beyond attendance

A sixteen-year-old maintains grades and caregiving responsibilities but reports exhaustion. Write a formulation that recognizes competence and overload together. Identify resources that are real but insufficient, and demands that could be shared or reduced.

Critique an evaluation that calls the young person resilient only because attendance is high. Add measures of distress, rest, support, and personally meaningful participation. Avoid treating help-seeking as a loss of resilience.

Design a review meeting in which the young person can disagree with adult praise. Identify a change that the family or school will make, not only a coping skill the young person must practice. Resilience work should expand support rather than increase the burden to appear strong.

## Exercise 17: Build two pathways to the same concern

Two children refuse school. One faces bullying; the other struggles with inaccessible written work and has developed panic around failure. Create separate timelines and formulations. Identify where the presenting behavior is similar and where the mechanisms differ.

For each case, specify an environmental action, a child-focused support, and a safety decision point. Explain why attendance alone is an incomplete outcome. Include learning access, distress, and the child’s sense of control.

Now imagine that both plans produce only partial improvement. Write a revision process that examines implementation, new information, and the original hypotheses. Avoid blaming the child for a plan that did not address the relevant pathway.

## Exercise 18: Individualize support after a diagnosis

A child receives an ADHD diagnosis. Design two support plans for different classroom environments. One classroom is noisy and uses long verbal instructions; the other provides predictable routines but little movement opportunity. Explain why the same label does not produce the same support plan.

Include the child’s preferences, a measure of participation, and a measure of experience. Identify which supports change the environment and which teach skills. Do not make access to support conditional on perfect behavior.

Write a question for an appropriate health professional and a separate question for the school team. Keep role boundaries clear. The diagnosis can inform care without becoming the entire description of the child.

## Exercise 19: Write an intervention revision rule

Choose a fictional intervention and identify its proposed mechanism. Specify the active practice, developmental adaptation, setting, and people involved. Then define implementation: what must actually happen for the intervention to have a fair opportunity to work?

Write one indicator of benefit, one possible burden, and one sign that the formulation may be wrong. Set a review date. State what would lead to adaptation, consultation, referral, or stopping the approach.

Explain how the child will participate in the review. A plan that measures only adult satisfaction may miss discomfort or lack of meaningful change. The revision rule protects the child from continued investment in an approach that is not helping.

## Exercise 20: Complete an integrated formulation and reflection

Select one fictional case from the workbook. Write a two-page formulation containing a timeline, strengths, competing hypotheses, evidence sources, cultural and structural context, the child’s goals, safety considerations, and a proportionate plan. Include a monitoring table and a review date.

Write a one-page caregiver explanation in simple English. Avoid blame, diagnostic certainty beyond the evidence, and predictions about the child’s entire future. Explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and what will happen next.

Finally, write a professional reflection. Identify an assumption you made, information you initially overlooked, and a way supervision or consultation could improve the decision. Distinguish routine care from research and identify when ethics approval would be required. The final product should demonstrate accountable judgment rather than the number of theories or techniques you can name.

## Portfolio review guide

A completed portfolio should show progression from description to explanation, from explanation to action, and from action to review. The reader should be able to see where each claim came from and how it could be revised. Strong work includes the child’s perspective, structural conditions, measurement limits, and a realistic account of implementation.

Use four review questions. First, is the reasoning clear enough that another professional can follow it? Second, does the evidence support the strength of the conclusion? Third, does the plan protect dignity, safety, and meaningful participation? Fourth, is there a credible way to learn that the plan is not working?

A portfolio is not a clinical record unless it is created within an authorized care system under appropriate rules. It is not research evidence merely because it contains numbers. It is a structured opportunity to practice the habits that responsible developmental counseling requires.
